Some of the most valuable software work never shows up in public product screenshots.

Internal tools sit closer to the actual mechanics of how teams operate. When they are good, they reduce friction, improve clarity, and remove manual work that people have quietly accepted for too long.

At BibleProject and in other workflow-heavy environments, I built Python-based internal tooling and AI-assisted support flows aimed at exactly that kind of leverage.

The goals were practical:

- reduce communication overhead
- support day-to-day operational workflows
- make useful information easier to access
- improve consistency without adding bureaucracy

This kind of work fits how I like to build software. It is less about novelty and more about precision. You are looking for the points where a small amount of tooling can unlock a large amount of execution.

It also requires a strong translation layer between technical systems and human systems. Internal software only works if it reflects how people actually operate, not how an engineer wishes they operated.

That is one reason I take AI tooling seriously as an engineering multiplier. Used well, it can accelerate prototyping, documentation, debugging, and workflow automation. Used poorly, it creates noise. The hard part is not access to the tool. It is judgment about where it belongs.
